# Design thinking and methodology

The rationale here is grounded in the implemented workflow, its test records and the research-quality problems identified during development. It explains commercial decisions without publishing the implementation.

## Start with the sales decision

The central question is whether there is a defensible reason to investigate a company further. Producing a longer list is not a useful outcome if the salesperson cannot explain why the accounts belong on it.

That led to a distinction between four ideas: the company operates in a relevant area; a seller product might support a particular workflow; there is a reason to investigate now; and the buyer actually intends to purchase. Evidence for one does not establish the others. The report keeps these distinctions visible so that a promising observation does not quietly become a qualified opportunity.

## Understand the seller before interpreting the buyer

A generic company summary does not establish product relevance. Research needs a clear understanding of what the seller's products do and the work they are intended to support. The next question is whether the account has a corresponding operational requirement.

This makes product selection an argument that needs support. An existing technical solution may weaken the opportunity, suggest a complementary use case or leave the situation unresolved. It is a qualification question rather than an automatic reason to recommend replacement.

## Separate source statements from sales hypotheses

A company webpage can establish what the company publicly says. It does not independently prove an outcome, a current problem or demand for a different supplier. The workflow therefore keeps cited statements, commercial hypotheses, conflicts and unknowns distinct.

Dates also have different meanings. A recent publication can discuss an older project, and retrieving a page today does not make its contents a new business event. Historical work may demonstrate relevance while saying little about present timing.

The salesperson should be able to trace a material finding back to a source, assess whether the wording supports the interpretation and decide what still needs to be asked.

## Research coverage is part of the result

An early failure was to treat available text as though it represented adequate research. A generic homepage might describe an industry without revealing technical work. A training page might describe a subsidiary or learner experience without establishing the parent company's production methods.

A missing finding therefore has two possible explanations: the account may be commercially irrelevant, or the investigation may not have reached the right material. Those explanations require different next steps. The adaptive extension adds coverage checks and a bounded opportunity to investigate a consequential public-information gap. Its live research quality is still unverified.

The stopping principle is practical: pursue information that could change the decision, then report remaining limits. Do not keep searching simply to obtain a positive recommendation.

## Keep counterevidence in the commercial story

Existing equipment, an established facility or a completed project can change the sales conversation. Removing these findings would produce a more flattering but less useful account profile.

The intended output is a balanced argument: why this account, why this product, whether timing is supported, what weakens the case and which unanswered question matters most. A hold is an acceptable recommendation. Missing information is not treated as evidence that the company lacks a capability.

## Use tests to challenge judgment

The saved research exposed different kinds of failure. Useful material was sometimes not retrieved, sometimes ignored after retrieval and sometimes lost during passage validation. Training-unit material also obscured the scope of the account. These required different corrections; a more capable model alone did not solve them all.

A controlled comparison reused the same evidence to limit changes in the underlying research material. It found more specific assessments for two accounts, but the overall result remained mixed. That supports a limited improvement claim rather than a general claim of superior research.

| Decision | Reason | Trade-off |
|---|---|---|
| Keep unknowns and holds explicit | Avoid false qualification | The result can contain fewer actionable accounts |
| Retain source passages and dates | Make claims reviewable | Presence of a quote does not guarantee correct interpretation |
| Look for existing alternatives | Improve the qualification conversation | A superficially attractive prospect may become less attractive |
| Bound further research | Control cost and unproductive investigation | Some public evidence may remain undiscovered |
| Test with retained evidence | Compare decisions on a stable basis | It does not test fresh source discovery |

## What success would mean

The next benchmark should assess useful source coverage, supported claims, appropriate product reasoning, false signals, consequential questions, cost and time. Independent review matters because a successfully completed execution can still produce a weak sales recommendation.

Earlier bounded integrations were live-tested. The adaptive extension has local and simulated-provider verification. No measured sales conversion, revenue uplift or reliable buying-intent detection is claimed.
